In July 2024 the American Bar Association issued [Formal Opinion 512](https://www.lawnext.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf), its first ethics guidance on lawyers' use of generative AI. It does not create new rules. It explains how the existing Model Rules apply. Any firm using AI, even occasionally, should have a written policy that reflects it. This is general information, not legal advice, and your state's rules and ethics opinions govern your practice.

## What the opinion covers

The opinion walks through the duties most affected by generative AI:

- **Competence (Rule 1.1).** Lawyers need a reasonable understanding of the benefits and risks of the tools they use, including that output can be wrong. That understanding has to keep up as the tools change.
- **Confidentiality (Rule 1.6).** Putting client information into a tool can risk disclosure or unauthorized access. The opinion concludes that because many self-learning tools can lead, directly or indirectly, to disclosure of client information, a client's informed consent is required before information relating to the representation is entered into one. Consent must be informed, meaning the client understands why the tool is used, the specific risks, and the benefits. General boilerplate in an engagement letter is not sufficient. Using a tool for something that involves no client information, such as brainstorming, does not need consent. Lawyers should also read the tool's terms of use and privacy policy first.
- **Communication (Rule 1.4).** Clients may need to be told when AI is used, depending on the circumstances and how significant the use is to the representation.
- **Candor and meritorious claims (Rules 3.1, 3.3).** Lawyers must review AI output for accuracy before it goes into a filing. Fabricated citations have already led to sanctions in real cases.
- **Supervision (Rules 5.1, 5.3).** Partners and managers must set policies and train lawyers and staff on responsible AI use.
- **Fees (Rule 1.5).** Fees must be reasonable. Time billed should reflect the work actually done, and general overhead costs of an AI tool are not usually billable to the client. The ABA's article on [fees and AI](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/law_practice/resources/law-practice-today/2024/december-2024/the-reasonableness-of-fees-when-using-ai/) goes deeper.

## An outline for a firm policy

A useful policy is short enough to read. A workable structure:

1. **Purpose and scope.** Who it covers (lawyers, paralegals, staff, contractors) and what counts as an AI tool.
2. **Approved tools.** A named list, with the plan or tier, and who approves additions. Everything else is not allowed for client work.
3. **What may be entered.** Classify information. State what is never entered, what may be entered only after redaction, and what is acceptable in an approved tool. Record the client's informed consent before any client information goes into a self-learning tool.
4. **Redaction first.** Describe the process for removing names, account numbers, and other identifying details before text reaches a tool that is not under the firm's control, and who checks it.
5. **Verification.** Every citation, quotation, and factual claim from AI is checked against a primary source by a lawyer before use.
6. **Client communication.** When to tell clients, when to get written consent, and how to record it.
7. **Billing.** How AI-assisted work is described and billed.
8. **Training and review.** Initial training, periodic refreshers, and a scheduled review of the policy and the tool list.
9. **Incidents.** What to do if confidential information is entered by mistake, including who to notify.

## The tool question

Not every tool carries the same risk. Questions to ask any vendor: Is our data used to train models? How long is it retained? Who at the vendor can see it? Where is it processed? What do the contract terms say about confidentiality? Answers differ by product and plan, so check the current terms for the exact offering you intend to use.

For matters that are especially sensitive, many firms prefer a setup where identifying details are removed before text leaves the firm, or where the model runs inside the firm's own environment.

## Bottom line

Opinion 512 asks for what a careful firm would do anyway: understand the tools, protect client information, check the output, supervise the people, and be honest with clients. A written policy turns that into daily practice. If you want help drafting one, or setting up redaction so sensitive details never reach an outside tool, book a consult.
